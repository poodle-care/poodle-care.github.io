Addison's Disease in Standard Poodles: Real Risk Numbers, Age at Diagnosis and Lifetime Treatment Costs (Zycortal vs Fludrocortisone)

Published: 2026-10-12 · Poodle Health

Roughly one in twelve dogs in the largest breed survey ever run had it, which makes Addison's disease in Standard Poodles one of the most common serious hormone disorders this breed faces. If your vet has just said "Addison's," you are now in a long game: a lifelong treatment plan, regular blood tests and a choice between two very different drugs. This guide puts the real risk numbers, the typical age at diagnosis and the cost of each treatment side by side, so you can see which option wins for your dog and your budget.

Quick answer (TL;DR)

  • Risk: a US survey of 1,722 Standard Poodles found 8.6% affected, and the trait's heritability was estimated at 0.75. Swedish insurance data put the yearly-average incidence at 0.42% for the breed versus 0.03% for all breeds, about 14 times higher.
  • Age: most dogs are diagnosed as young or middle-aged adults (around 4 years old on average across breeds), but it can appear from puppyhood to about 12 years.
  • Cost duel: for a 50 lb Standard Poodle, Zycortal injections at the label dose cost about $2,600 a year in drug alone at current online prices. Fludrocortisone tablets cost about $700 to $1,200 a year, depending on where you fill them. Lower Zycortal doses and longer intervals, adjusted by your vet, can narrow the gap a lot.
  • Outcome: with proper treatment, most dogs have a good to excellent prognosis and live normal lives.

How common is Addison's disease in Standard Poodles?

Much more common than in the average dog: survey and insurance data put Standard Poodles in the top risk group of all breeds, with estimates ranging from under 1% to nearly 9% depending on how the data were collected.

Addison's disease (primary hypoadrenocorticism) happens when the immune system destroys the outer layer of the adrenal glands. The dog loses cortisol, the stress hormone, and usually aldosterone, the hormone that keeps sodium and potassium in balance. The University of Minnesota canine genetics lab describes it as an immune-mediated disease that requires "lifelong commitment by owners with hormone replacement therapy (typically a monthly injection and daily pills)."

The welfare charity UFAW summarizes the key survey this way: Famula and colleagues surveyed 1,722 Standard Poodles and found 8.6% had hypoadrenocorticism, with a "very high heritability of 0.75." A heritability of 0.75 means that about three quarters of the variation in who gets sick comes down to genes. That is why breeders in this breed take it so seriously.

Why a genetic test still doesn't exist

Early work suspected a single recessive gene, but later research points to many genes acting together. A 2016 study from North Carolina State University compared affected and unaffected Standard Poodles using SNP arrays and whole-genome sequencing and still did not find a single clear marker. The authors concluded it is likely a complex inherited disorder. For now there is no DNA test you can order. The only "test" a breeder has is a pedigree with as few affected relatives as possible.

At what age are Standard Poodles diagnosed with Addison's?

Usually as young to middle-aged adults: the average age at diagnosis across dogs is about 4 years, but cases have been found from puppies up to about 12 years old.

According to the Wikipedia overview of canine hypoadrenocorticism, the average age at diagnosis is four years, "although it has been found in puppies and dogs up to twelve years old." Swedish data on Standard Poodles suggested the breed may be diagnosed slightly younger than the general literature describes. In the North Carolina genetics study, the researchers did not accept a dog as "unaffected" until it was at least 10 years old. That tells you how late the disease can still appear.

Males vs females: a tie in this breed

Across all dogs, about 70% of Addison's cases are female. In Standard Poodles the 2003 survey found the sexes were affected equally. So owning a male does not make you safe. A 2023 case-control survey of Standard Poodles and Portuguese Water Dogs found that neutered males had six times the odds of disease compared with intact males (adjusted OR 6.0), and spayed females 2.5 times the odds compared with intact females. The authors stress these are associations, not proof that neutering causes Addison's.

Why diagnosis often comes late

Addison's is called "the great pretender." Early signs come and go: a picky appetite, an occasional vomit, a dog that seems tired after a normal walk. More than half of affected dogs show mild, waxing-and-waning lethargy, poor appetite, vomiting, weakness or weight loss before diagnosis. Some dogs are only diagnosed during an Addisonian crisis, when they collapse in shock and need emergency care. If your Poodle also has a history of stomach trouble, remember that vague GI signs can overlap with other breed problems such as bloat in deep-chested Poodles, which is a different emergency with different treatment.

How is Addison's diagnosed, and what does testing cost?

Veterinarian Dr. Dan walks through the two forms of Addison's disease (typical, with the sodium and potassium imbalance, and atypical, where only cortisol is missing), the signs that lead to testing and how lifelong treatment works. It is a good primer before the ACTH test conversation with your vet.

The definitive test is an ACTH stimulation test, which usually costs $150 to $300 and takes an hour or two at your vet's clinic.

A routine blood panel often gives the first clue: high potassium, low sodium, or a low sodium-to-potassium ratio. Your vet may then check a resting (baseline) cortisol. A normal or high value effectively rules Addison's out. A low value leads to the ACTH stimulation test. PetMD's guide to the ACTH stim test puts the test at $150 to $300 and calls it the best diagnostic test for Addison's in dogs. Dogs with Addison's barely respond to the injected hormone, so cortisol stays low before and after.

Avoid steroids before the test

Prednisone and similar drugs can falsely raise cortisol readings. If your dog is stable, your vet will usually ask you to avoid steroids for at least 24 hours before testing. In a crisis, the priority is emergency stabilization with fluids and hormone support, and your vet will plan testing around it.

Zycortal vs fludrocortisone: which treatment wins?

Neither wins for every dog. Zycortal (a DOCP injection) gives tighter, separate control of each hormone; fludrocortisone tablets are much cheaper for big dogs but cause more side effects in some.

Every Addisonian dog needs two hormones replaced. Mineralocorticoid replaces aldosterone, and glucocorticoid replaces cortisol. The two main options work very differently:

Round 1: control and side effects

Because Zycortal only replaces aldosterone, your vet can fine-tune the prednisone dose separately. That matters because too much glucocorticoid causes heavy drinking, urination and hunger. Today's Veterinary Practice reviews the DOCP studies. The label dose is 2.2 mg/kg every 25 days, but a 2019 study found a 1.5 mg/kg starting dose of Zycortal was enough to control signs, and one 2017 study found the effect lasted 32 to 94 days (median 62 days) in individual dogs. Zycortal was found to be noninferior to Percorten-V, the older DOCP brand.

Fludrocortisone has a mild glucocorticoid effect, which is why some dogs can skip prednisone on it. It is also why polyuria, polydipsia and polyphagia are its classic, dose-dependent side effects. Dechra notes that some dogs cannot be well controlled on fludrocortisone, and that dogs switched to Zycortal did as well as dogs started on it.

Round 2: price

Here the tablet usually wins, especially for a large dog. At the time of writing, VetRxDirect lists a 4 mL vial of Zycortal (25 mg/mL) at $355.88, or $338.09 on autoship. Fludrocortisone 0.1 mg is listed at $0.74 per tablet at 1-800-PetMeds, and human-pharmacy discount coupons can bring 30 tablets down to about $12.54 (about $0.42 each) at some chains. For a 50 lb Standard Poodle, that is how the numbers stack up:

  • Zycortal, label dose (2.2 mg/kg every 25 days): 2 mL per shot, 2 shots per vial, about 7.3 vials a year, or about $2,600/year.
  • Zycortal, 1.5 mg/kg every 25 days: about 1.36 mL per shot, about 5 vials a year, or about $1,770/year. At every 30 days, it drops to about $1,470/year.
  • Fludrocortisone at 0.009 mg/lb/day: 4.5 tablets a day, about 1,640 tablets a year, or about $690/year at $0.42 per tablet and about $1,215/year at $0.74.

These are drug-only numbers. Prednisone is cheap, but blood tests are not, so add your clinic's price for electrolyte rechecks. Use the calculator below with your own dog's weight and your own prices.

What does lifelong Addison's care look like month to month?

One injection or a daily pill, a daily low-dose steroid for most dogs, and blood tests that start frequent and then settle to a few times a year.

Monitoring timeline

Dechra, the maker of Zycortal, recommends checking the sodium/potassium ratio at 10 and 25 days after the first injection. Elanco's protocol for Percorten-V uses day 14 and day 25. Your vet uses these results to set the dose and the interval. For fludrocortisone, the Wedgewood veterinary monograph advises weekly electrolyte, BUN and creatinine checks at the start until the dog is stable. Once your dog is well controlled, most vets settle into periodic rechecks, plus extra tests if your dog seems unwell.

Stress days need extra steroid

An Addisonian dog cannot make its own cortisol when stressed. Ask your vet for a written "stress dose" plan for boarding, travel, surgery, shows or a big change at home. Standard Poodles that struggle when left alone are a good example: if your dog has separation anxiety, a kennel stay can count as a stress event worth planning for.

Exercise and normal life

VCA Hospitals notes that the vast majority of dogs with Addison's have a good to excellent prognosis once stabilized, and most resume normal lives. Diet and activity can often stay the same. Once your vet is happy with bloodwork, your Poodle can usually return to the normal daily exercise a Standard Poodle needs. Just watch for unusual fatigue on hot or demanding days.

Step-by-step checklist after an Addison's diagnosis

  1. Get a copy of the ACTH stim results and the electrolyte panel for your records. You will compare future tests against them.
  2. Ask which mineralocorticoid your vet recommends and why. Ask about the dose in mg/kg and the planned interval.
  3. Run the numbers in the calculator above with your real prices. Ask your vet if they will write a prescription you can fill at an online or human pharmacy.
  4. Book the first two rechecks (around day 10–14 and day 25 for Zycortal) before you leave the clinic.
  5. Learn to give the injection at home if you choose Zycortal. Many owners do, which cuts the cost and stress of clinic visits.
  6. Write down a stress-dose plan for prednisone and keep a few extra tablets in your travel kit.
  7. Set reminders for daily pills and the shot date. A missed dose leaves your dog without hormones it can no longer make.
  8. Tell the breeder. Because the disease is highly heritable, breeders need to know about affected dogs to make better pairing decisions.

Common mistakes owners make with Addison's

  • Stopping prednisone because the dog "looks fine." On Zycortal, prednisone is the dog's only cortisol source. Never stop it without your vet.
  • Treating label dose as the only option. Ask whether a lower dose or longer interval is safe for your dog, guided by electrolytes. It is the biggest single cost lever.
  • Skipping rechecks to save money. Dose changes based on bloodwork are what allow cost cuts safely. Without data, vets stay on the cautious, higher dose.
  • Ignoring heavy drinking and urination on fludrocortisone. These are dose-dependent side effects. Report them, because a dose change or switch may help.
  • Forgetting stress days. Surgery, boarding and travel without extra steroid can trigger a crisis.
  • Assuming males are low risk. In Standard Poodles, both sexes are affected about equally.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of Standard Poodles get Addison's disease?

A US survey of 1,722 Standard Poodles found 8.6% affected. Swedish insurance data showed a lower but still high average incidence of 0.42% over seven years, versus 0.03% for all breeds. The true lifetime risk likely lies somewhere in that range.

At what age do Standard Poodles usually get Addison's?

Most dogs are diagnosed as young to middle-aged adults, with an average of around 4 years across dogs. Cases have been reported from puppies up to about 12 years, so a clear history at age 5 does not rule it out.

Is Zycortal or fludrocortisone cheaper for a Standard Poodle?

For a large dog, fludrocortisone tablets are usually cheaper. A 50 lb dog costs roughly $700 to $1,200 a year on tablets versus $1,500 to $2,600 a year on Zycortal, depending on dose and interval. Bloodwork costs come on top of both.

Can a dog with Addison's live a normal life?

Yes. Once stabilized on treatment, most dogs have a good to excellent prognosis and return to normal diet and activity. Lifelong medication and regular blood tests are required.

Is there a genetic test for Addison's in Standard Poodles?

No. Heritability is high (about 0.75), but the disease appears to involve many genes, and no single marker has been found. Research groups, including the University of Minnesota, are still working on it.

Does my dog still need prednisone with Zycortal?

Most dogs do. Zycortal replaces only aldosterone, so daily prednisolone or prednisone (usually starting at 0.2–0.4 mg/kg) replaces cortisol. Your vet will adjust it to the lowest dose that keeps your dog well.

Can I switch from fludrocortisone to Zycortal?

Yes, with your vet's guidance. Dechra reports that dogs switched from fludrocortisone did as well on Zycortal as dogs started on it at diagnosis. A switch is common when dogs have heavy thirst and urination on tablets.

The bottom line

Addison's disease in Standard Poodles is common, highly heritable and usually shows up in young adult dogs. The good news is that it is very treatable. In the cost duel, fludrocortisone usually wins on price for a big dog, while Zycortal often wins on control and fewer thirst-related side effects. Run your own numbers in the calculator, then decide with your vet which plan your Poodle can stay on for the long game.
