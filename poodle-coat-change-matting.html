Coat changes

Poodle Coat Change: Why Your Poodle Suddenly Mats Every Day at 9-18 Months and a Week-by-Week Brushing Plan to Get Through It

Published 2026-11-08

The Poodle coat change is the stretch, usually somewhere between 9 and 18 months, when a puppy who brushed out in ten easy minutes starts growing fresh mats every single day. If that is your dog right now, you have not suddenly become bad at brushing. The coat itself changed under your hands, and this guide gives you a week-by-week plan to get through it without shaving the dog unless you choose to.

Quick answer: During the change, a thicker adult coat grows in underneath the soft puppy coat, and the two tangle together so fast that mats can appear almost overnight, mostly on the ears, neck, shoulders and under the elbows. The fix is daily line brushing down to the skin on damp, clean hair, a comb check after every section, and a short daily sweep of the hotspots. If you cannot give that time for a few months, a shorter clip is a sensible choice, not a failure.

Why does my Poodle suddenly mat every day?

Because the coat is changing from puppy to adult texture, and for a while your dog wears both at once. The soft puppy hair stays in the coat while the denser adult hair grows in beneath it, and that mix knots far faster than either coat on its own.

A long-running grooming tutorial on brushing a Poodle's coat describes exactly what owners see: a puppy from 4 to 10 months usually needs brushing about twice a week, then sometime between 10 and 14 months the coat starts to change and the hair "almost seems to mat overnight", especially on the ears, the neck and shoulders, and under the elbows. The top coat can look sparse and straggly while the hair underneath feels much thicker.

Why the timing feels so sudden

You do not see the adult coat arriving, because it starts at the skin. The surface still looks like the puppy coat you are used to, so a quick brush over the top feels fine, while the knots build underneath. By the time you can see a mat, it has usually been forming for a few days. That is why owners say it happened overnight, and it is why surface brushing stops working at this age.

It is not your technique, and you are not alone

The same tutorial calls the change "exasperating" and says it is usually the point when many owners decide they are not meant to be Poodle exhibitors and cut the dog down. People who show Poodles for years still find these months hard. If you feel out of your depth right now, that is the coat, not you. You can do this, and the steps are learnable.

When does the Poodle coat change start and end?

For most Poodles the change sits somewhere in the 9 to 18 month window, with the first daily mats often showing up around 10 to 14 months. How long it lasts depends on the individual coat: some dogs are through it in a few weeks, others take months.

The grooming tutorial linked above notes that white and light-colored dogs, and dogs with fine-textured, fragile coats, can take longer to change. If you want the full month-by-month picture from birth, our Poodle puppy coat change timeline walks through each stage. This guide focuses on the part most owners struggle with: the daily mats and how to brush through them.

Signs your dog has entered the change

  • Mats appear between brushings that used to stay clean for a week.
  • The coat near the skin feels thicker or coarser than the tips.
  • The top looks thinner or uneven while the base feels dense.
  • Mats show up first behind the ears, on the neck, in the armpits and under the elbows.

Signs the change is finishing

You are near the end when two or three full comb-throughs in a row find only a knot or two, and when a day without brushing no longer leaves you with a new mat. That is the point to start tapering the plan below, not before.

The weekly-plus-daily rhythm for adult coats comes from the grooming sheet that Guide Dogs of the Desert gives its Poodle handlers: full grooming at least once a week, including ears and teeth, maintenance grooming daily, and a reminder that the longer the hair, the more work it takes to maintain.

What is line brushing, and why does it stop the mats?

Line brushing means parting the coat down to the skin and brushing one thin layer at a time, from the skin outwards, before moving the part a little further along. It is the only method that reaches the base of the coat, which is where coat-change mats start.

The grooming tutorial describes the classic version: part the hair lengthwise, brush the layer, comb it, then make another part about an inch below and repeat until one whole side is done. The details below make the difference between a session that works and one that just fluffs the top.

Mist first, never brush dry

The tutorial is firm on this: never brush the hair dry. Dry brushing creates static that can snap off the hair ends and encourages mats to form. A light mist of water with a little conditioner or a coat spray is enough. You do not need to soak the coat, just dampen the layer you are working on.

Brush clean hair

Dirty hair is harder to brush and tangles more easily. If the coat is both dirty and matted, the same source recommends bathing first, letting a heavy creme rinse or tangle remover soak in, then working the mats out of clean hair while a strong dryer blows on it.

Check every line with a comb

The brush tells you very little; the comb tells you the truth. After each layer, run a wide-tooth comb from the outside in towards the skin. If it glides, the layer is finished. If it catches, that is a knot you would have missed, and it will be bigger tomorrow.

Split mats, do not rip them

When you find a mat, separate it into smaller sections with your fingers before you brush each piece. The Guide Dogs of the Desert sheet adds two practical rules for combing out mats: go slowly from the bottom and work up, and hold a finger on the skin to help prevent pulling. It also tells handlers to ask their groomer for help with really bad mats.

A week-by-week brushing plan for the coat change

This plan is built for the months of daily mats. It assumes a normal family dog in a moderate clip, not a show coat. Change the minutes to fit your dog, using the planner above, and keep the order of the steps.

Week 1: reset the coat

  1. Bathe the dog, use a conditioner and leave it in for a few minutes before rinsing.
  2. Blow dry thoroughly, brushing as you dry. Clean, straight-dried hair shows every knot.
  3. Do one complete line-brush of every zone and comb each layer. Count the mats you find and write the number down.
  4. Decide on length. If you found felted patches or more mats than you can work out calmly, book a groomer for a shorter body clip and start week 2 on the short coat.

Weeks 2 to 4: daily sweep plus one zone

  1. Every day, do a short sweep of the hotspots: behind the ears, the ear leather, the neck and collar line, the armpits and under the elbows.
  2. Each weekday, line brush one or two of the other zones: shoulders and back, sides and belly, hind legs and rear, front legs and feet, tail and topknot.
  3. Once a week, do a full line-brush of the whole dog and finish with a comb-through. Write the mat count down again.
  4. If the dog gets wet or wears a harness for a long walk, brush the damp or rubbed areas the same day.

Weeks 5 to 8: the peak

For many dogs the worst weeks come here, when there is the most adult hair mixed with the most leftover puppy hair. Keep the daily sweep, and if the weekly count is still rising, add a second short pass on the hotspots in the evening. If the count rises two weeks in a row despite the plan, that is a clear sign the coat is longer than your schedule can carry right now, and a shorter clip is the kind decision.

Week 9 and after: taper slowly

When two or three weekly comb-throughs in a row come back with one knot or none, stretch the body zones to every other day and keep the daily hotspot sweep a little longer. Once a full week passes without new mats, move to the adult rhythm: a full groom at least weekly and short daily maintenance. If you are working out how much weekly time an adult clip will take, our guide to Poodle grooming time by clip style compares the hours.

A simple log keeps you honest

Write three numbers after each full comb-through: the date, the mat count and the minutes it took. Over a few weeks, the trend tells you whether the plan is working far better than your memory of a bad Tuesday. If you like paper, the free Poodle grooming planner gives you a page to track it.

Should I shave my Poodle during the coat change?

You do not have to, but a shorter clip is a reasonable choice if you cannot brush daily for the next few months. Keeping the length needs daily line brushing; going short trades the fluffy look for far less daily work while the adult coat grows in.

Neither choice says anything about how good an owner you are. Exhibitors keep the coat because they need it for the ring; most pet owners simply want a dog without mats. The table compares the two paths.

How short is short?

For a working-dog style puppy or kennel cut, the Guide Dogs of the Desert sheet lists a #7 blade on the body in summer and a #5 or #4 blade in winter (the higher the number, the shorter the hair), a #10 on the face and feet, a face, feet and hygiene clip about every 3 weeks and a full visit about every 6 weeks. Your groomer will adjust for your dog, but those numbers give you a clear starting point to ask for. Where do coat-change mats hide?

They start where the coat moves or rubs most: behind and under the ears, along the neck and collar line, in the armpits and under the elbows, and wherever a harness sits. Check these spots every day, even on days you skip the rest.

The grooming tutorial asks you to check behind the ears, on the neck and in the armpits before you turn the dog over to do the other side, because those spots hide tangles that the body brushing misses. For a zone-by-zone map that also changes with clip style, see our Poodle matting hotspots body map.

Ears need a gentler stroke

The Guide Dogs of the Desert sheet tells handlers to hold the ear in one hand and brush down, never sideways, then flip the ear over and gently brush the underside. The ear leather is thin and sensitive, so let the comb, not force, find the knots there.

Common mistakes during the Poodle coat change

  • Brushing only the top. The surface looks fine while the base felts. If you cannot see skin at the part, you have not reached the mat.
  • Brushing dry hair. Static breaks the ends and helps new mats form. Mist each layer first.
  • Skipping the comb check. A slicker can glide over a small knot. The comb catches it while it is still small.
  • Saving it all for the weekend. One long session cannot undo six days of new knots. Short daily work beats a heroic Sunday.
  • Bathing and air drying a matted coat. A bath does not remove knots on its own. Use a conditioner and work the mats out of clean hair while a dryer blows on it, as the tutorial describes.
  • Pulling a mat out in one go. Split it with your fingers, hold the skin, and work from the bottom up.
  • Treating a short clip as defeat. It is a tool for a hard few months. The adult coat still grows in under it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does the Poodle coat change last?

It varies from dog to dog. Some Poodles get through it in a few weeks, while others, especially white and light-colored dogs and dogs with fine, fragile coats, take longer. The first daily mats often appear between about 10 and 14 months.

How often should I brush my Poodle during the coat change?

Daily. Mats can form almost overnight during the change, so plan on a short hotspot sweep every day, one or two body zones on most days and a full line-brush with a comb check once a week.

Why does my Poodle mat more after a bath?

A bath does not remove knots that were already there, and if the coat air dries with them, they are harder to find and brush out. Bathe with a conditioner, then dry the coat with a dryer while you brush, so you can work out knots in clean hair.

Is a slicker brush or a pin brush better for the coat change?

The grooming tutorial cited above uses a pin brush on the longer body coat and a fine-wire slicker on shorter hair such as the legs, and a wide-tooth comb to check for tangles. The Guide Dogs of the Desert handlers' sheet uses a soft slicker and a comb with wide and fine teeth. Whatever brush you use, the comb check is what tells you a layer is done.

Should I shave my Poodle to get through the coat change?

Only if daily brushing is not realistic for you or the coat already has felted patches. A shorter body clip makes the months of the change far easier, and the adult coat still grows in underneath it.

When can I stop brushing every day?

When two or three weekly comb-throughs in a row find one knot or none. Taper the body zones to every other day first, keep the daily hotspot sweep a little longer, then move to a weekly full groom with short daily maintenance.

The bottom line

The Poodle coat change is a temporary stage, not a sign that you are doing something wrong. For a few months the puppy coat and the adult coat tangle together, so the coat needs daily line brushing down to the skin, a comb check on every layer and a quick sweep of the hotspots. Follow the week-by-week plan, keep a simple mat count, and if your schedule cannot carry the length, choose a shorter clip without guilt. On the other side of the change is an adult coat that is much easier to live with.
