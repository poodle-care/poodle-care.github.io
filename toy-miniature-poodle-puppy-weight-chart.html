Toy or Miniature Poodle Puppy? Week-by-Week Weight Benchmarks to Predict Adult Size

Published 2026-11-09

Every Saturday the puppy goes on the kitchen scale, and every Saturday the same question comes back: is this a toy or miniature Poodle puppy, and how big will this one really get? If the breeder said "probably a Toy" and the puppy keeps outgrowing the harness, you are not imagining things, and you are not the first owner to wonder. This guide gives you honest week-by-week weight benchmarks, a calculator built on a real Toy Poodle growth study, and the one measurement that actually settles the question.

Quick answer: Toy and Miniature are defined by height at the shoulder, not weight: a Toy is 10 inches or under, a Miniature is over 10 and up to 15 inches. Weight is still a good early clue. In a study that weighed 65 Toy Poodle puppies every week, puppies reached about half of their adult weight at 11 to 12 weeks, so a 12-week weight roughly doubled gives a first estimate of adult weight. A puppy heading for under about 7 lb is most likely a Toy, one heading for 10 to 15 lb is most likely a Miniature, and the zone in between can only be settled with a tape measure once growth slows.

One honest warning before the details: a projection from one weigh-in is a guess with a number attached. The value comes from repeating it. Three or four weekly weights that keep pointing to the same adult weight tell you far more than any single reading, and far more than a seller's label.

Because the final answer is a height, it helps to see how that height is measured. In this short video from the Dog Advisory Council you will see where the withers are on a dog and how to measure from that point to the floor, which is exactly the measurement the Poodle standard uses.

What decides whether a Poodle is a Toy or a Miniature?

Height at the highest point of the shoulders decides it, nothing else. The breed standard sets the Toy at 10 inches or under and the Miniature at over 10 inches and up to 15 inches, and it does not mention weight at all.

The Poodle Club of America breed standard spells it out: the Miniature is "15 inches or under at the highest point of the shoulders, with a minimum height in excess of 10 inches", the Toy is "10 inches or under", and the Standard is over 15 inches. The Toy standard is the same as for the other two varieties except for height. All three are one breed with the same build, so a Toy is simply a smaller copy of the same dog, not a different kind of Poodle.

Why weight is still the clue owners use

You cannot measure the adult height of an 8-week-old puppy, but you can weigh it in ten seconds. Weight changes week to week in a fairly predictable curve, and adult weight and adult height move together closely enough that weight is a useful early signal. It just is not the definition. Two Poodles of the same weight can sit on different sides of the 10-inch line if one is leaner and leggier.

The typical weight ranges

For Miniatures, the AKC breed profile lists 10 to 15 pounds and 10 to 15 inches. Toy weights vary more than the short "4 to 6 pounds" you often see quoted. In the Italian Toy Poodle study used for the calculator, the adult dogs averaged 3,208 grams, about 7.1 lb, with a standard deviation of 860 grams, roughly 1.9 lb. That means plenty of healthy Toys from that breeding program weighed 5 to 9 lb as adults. The space between about 7 and 10 lb is where Toy and Miniature overlap, and it is where most of the confusion comes from.

What about "teacup" and "royal" sizes?

The standard names only three varieties: Standard, Miniature and Toy. Words like teacup, tiny toy or royal are sales labels, not varieties. A puppy sold as a teacup is, by the standard, a Toy, and the same 10-inch line applies.

How fast do Toy Poodle puppies grow?

Very fast early, then they level off. Toy Poodle puppies reach about 30% of their adult weight at around 7 weeks, about 50% at 11 to 12 weeks, and close to 99% somewhere between 35 and 51 weeks, depending on the growth model.

Those numbers come from a 2020 study in the Italian Journal of Animal Science by Menchetti, Padalino, Brasileiro Fernandes and Nanni Costa, which is one of the very few growth studies done on Toy Poodles specifically. The team followed 65 puppies (30 males and 35 females) born at one breeding center, recorded birth weight and then weight every week for 53 weeks, and also recorded the parents' weights, litter size, activity and management. Forty-six of the puppies were sold during the study, and their new owners kept reporting the weights.

What the study found

  • Birth weight: 154 ± 35 grams on average, ranging from 82 to 252 grams.
  • Adult weight: 3,208 ± 860 grams on average.
  • Halfway point: 50% of adult weight at 11 to 12 weeks overall.
  • Best curve: the Gompertz model fitted best, with a mean absolute percentage error of 1.9%, and the authors used it to draw centile charts for males and females.
  • What mattered for adult size: birth weight, sex and the mother's weight. Litter size, management and daily activity mattered less in this well-managed group.

Males and females grow on slightly different clocks

In the same study, males reached 50% of their adult weight at 10.9 to 11.2 weeks, and females took about a week longer and grew at a lower rate. That is why the calculator asks for sex: at the same age, a female has usually reached a slightly smaller share of her adult weight than a male, so the same puppy weight projects a little heavier for her.

An honest limit

All 65 puppies came from one Italian kennel with careful, standardized care, and the authors call their curve results preliminary. Your puppy's line may grow a little differently. Treat the curve as a good map, not a promise, and let your own weekly weights correct it.

How to read your puppy's row

Say your puppy weighs 3.5 lb at 10 weeks. In the 10-week row, 3.5 lb sits in the 8 lb adult column, which is right in the overlap zone. Weigh again at 12 and 14 weeks. If the puppy hits about 4.2 and 4.8 lb, the 8 lb track is holding, and the final answer will come from the tape measure. If it reaches 5.2 lb at 12 weeks instead, it has moved to the 10 lb column and Miniature becomes far more likely.

Can you predict adult size from an 8-week weight?

Roughly, yes, but the error is large. On the study curve an 8-week-old Toy Poodle carries about 35% of its adult weight, so multiplying the 8-week weight by about 2.8 gives a first estimate, and that estimate can easily be off by a pound or more on a small dog.

The reason is simple arithmetic. At 8 weeks, a difference of a few ounces between two puppies gets multiplied almost three times. Many puppies also go home at about this age, and the move, a new food and a few unsettled days can flatten the scale for a week. One reading at that point is not a verdict.

These multipliers are simply 1 divided by the share of adult weight in the benchmark table. The familiar breeder rule of doubling the weight at about three months lines up with the study's finding that Toy Poodles reach half their adult weight at 11 to 12 weeks.

What the parents tell you

Ask the breeder for the actual weights and heights of both parents, not just "they are Toys". In the study, the mother's weight was linked to the puppy's weight from 3 weeks on and to adult weight, while the father's weight had a weaker, less consistent effect. Birth weight also mattered at every age. If the mother is a 9 lb, 10.5-inch dog, a puppy from her that is tracking toward 8 lb is no surprise at all.

How to weigh and measure your puppy at home: a step-by-step routine

Good benchmarks need good numbers. This routine takes about five minutes a week and makes your log far more reliable.

  1. Pick one day and one time. Weigh before breakfast on the same weekday. A full stomach and a full bladder can add a noticeable share of a small puppy's weight.
  2. Use a kitchen or baby scale for small puppies. Under about 5 lb, a bathroom scale is too coarse. Put a small basket on the scale, tare it to zero, then add the puppy.
  3. Bathroom scale method for bigger puppies. Weigh yourself, then weigh yourself holding the puppy, and subtract. Do it twice and take the average.
  4. Write it down the same day. Age in weeks, weight and anything unusual: a new home, a missed meal, a bath. Put the numbers into the calculator above and keep the projection next to them.
  5. Start height checks at about 4 to 5 months. Stand the puppy four square on a hard floor, front legs straight under the shoulders and back legs even. Find the withers, the top of the shoulder blades, with your fingers, because the coat hides them.
  6. Use a level and a wall. Rest a ruler or small level flat on the withers, touching a wall, mark the wall and measure from the floor to the mark. Measure twice; if the two numbers differ by more than a quarter inch, the puppy moved.
  7. Compare trends, not single days. Look at the last three entries. If they agree, trust them. If one is an outlier, repeat it next week before changing your expectations.

Owners in a busy r/poodles thread, "Is she a mini or a toy?", ran into exactly this. One owner reported 10 inches at the shoulders and 12 inches at the withers, and experienced members pointed out that the shoulders and withers are the same point, so a 2-inch gap means the measuring needs a second look, with the dog standing square. Another reminded everyone that Poodles sometimes outgrow their official height category. It is a good example of how much a careful measurement matters right at the 10-inch line.

Common mistakes when predicting a Poodle puppy's adult size

  • Trusting the label more than the parents. "Toy" on a sales listing is a prediction. The parents' measured heights and weights are evidence.
  • Judging from one weigh-in. A single 8-week weight multiplied by almost three can swing the estimate across the Toy and Miniature line. Use at least three weekly weights.
  • Using weight as the final test. The variety is defined by height. A lean, leggy 8 lb dog and a compact 8 lb dog can land on different sides of 10 inches.
  • Measuring to the top of the head or the top of the coat. The standard measures at the highest point of the shoulders, at the bone, not at the fluff.
  • Measuring a puppy that is sitting, leaning or stretching. Uneven legs change the reading by an inch or more. Square the dog up every time.
  • Calling it too early. In the Toy study, puppies did not reach 99% of adult weight until 35 to 51 weeks. A puppy that looks done at five months can still add weight.
  • Changing the feeding plan to steer the size. Food does not change which variety a puppy belongs to. Ask your vet before changing the amount or type of food.

What if my "Toy" puppy turns out to be a Miniature?

Then you have a healthy small Poodle who is a little bigger than the label, and that is very common. Nothing about the dog you already love changes; only the size of the harness does.

It is worth saying plainly, because a lot of owners feel misled, embarrassed, or worried they did something wrong. You did not. The curve shows that early weights carry real uncertainty, and the overlap between the largest Toys and the smallest Miniatures is wide. The r/poodles thread above is full of people comparing notes on dogs right at the line, and the shared message is reassuring: most of them stopped caring about the label within months.

What actually changes with a bigger dog

Mostly practical things: crate and harness size, how much food goes in the bowl, and how far the dog can comfortably walk. A Miniature usually handles longer outings than a Toy. Our guide on how much exercise a Standard Poodle needs covers the larger end of the breed and shows how activity grows with size and age. Coat care stays the same in kind, and you can plan it with the free Poodle grooming planner whatever size your puppy turns out to be.

If you are still choosing a puppy

Ask to see both parents, ask for their measured heights, and ask what size earlier litters from the same pair reached as adults. A breeder who has those numbers and shares them openly is giving you the best prediction available. A breeder who promises an exact adult weight is promising more than anyone can know.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age can you tell if a Poodle is a Toy or a Miniature?

Weight gives a useful hint from about 12 weeks, when Toy Poodle puppies are near half their adult weight. A confident answer usually comes from a height measurement once growth has slowed, after about 6 to 9 months, because the variety is defined by height at the shoulder.

How much should a Toy Poodle puppy weigh at 8 weeks?

On the Toy Poodle growth curve, a puppy heading for a 4 to 6 lb adult weighs about 1.4 to 2.1 lb at 8 weeks, and one heading for an 8 lb adult weighs about 2.8 lb. Individual puppies vary, so compare several weekly weights rather than one.

Does the double-at-12-weeks rule work for Poodles?

It is a reasonable first estimate. In a study of 65 Toy Poodle puppies, the dogs reached 50% of adult weight at 11 to 12 weeks, so doubling the 12-week weight lands close to the curve. Males grow a little faster, so for them doubling can run slightly high.

Can a Toy Poodle weigh 10 pounds?

Yes. The Toy variety is defined by a height of 10 inches or under, not by weight. Most Toys are lighter, but weights vary widely: in a study of Toy Poodles the adults averaged about 7.1 lb with a standard deviation of about 1.9 lb, so a sturdy Toy near the 10-inch limit can come close to 10 lb.

When do Toy and Miniature Poodles stop growing?

In the Toy Poodle study, puppies reached 99% of their adult weight between about 35 and 51 weeks, so most of the growth is done by the first birthday. Miniatures follow a similar pattern and may finish a little later.

Is a teacup Poodle a separate variety?

No. The breed standard names three varieties only: Standard, Miniature and Toy. A puppy sold as a teacup is a small Toy by the standard, and the same 10-inch height limit applies.

The bottom line

Whether you have a toy or miniature Poodle puppy is finally a question for a tape measure: 10 inches at the shoulder is the line. Until the puppy is old enough to measure, weight is the best clue you have. Weigh weekly at the same time, compare the numbers with the benchmarks, double the 12-week weight for a first estimate, and look at the parents' real sizes. If the puppy ends up an inch or two bigger than the label, you have lost nothing that matters.
