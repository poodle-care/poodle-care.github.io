Energy & alone time

Standard Poodle Separation Anxiety: How Common Is It?

Published 2026-05-21

Standard Poodles are famously people-focused, and plenty of owners worry that this makes them prone to separation anxiety. The honest answer to "how common is it?" has two parts. Across dogs in general, serious separation problems affect a minority: about 5% of dogs in the largest recent survey, and up to about 14–20% in earlier studies that used looser definitions. For Standard Poodles specifically, no published study gives a reliable breed figure. What the research does show is which traits tend to travel with separation problems, and the ASPCA explains how to tell real separation distress from boredom and how to build up time alone step by step.

Quick answer: In a survey of 13,715 Finnish dogs published in Scientific Reports in 2020, about 5% showed frequent separation-related behaviour, the least common of the seven anxiety traits studied. Earlier studies cited in the same paper put separation anxiety at 14–20% of dogs. There is no trustworthy Poodle-only figure. The ASPCA says true separation distress shows up only when the dog is alone or about to be, usually within minutes of you leaving, and is treated by very gradual practice absences, never punishment.

How common is separation anxiety in dogs?

The largest recent survey on the subject is the Finnish study by Salonen and colleagues, published in Scientific Reports in 2020. Owners of 13,715 dogs from 264 breeds answered a detailed behaviour questionnaire. Separation-related behaviour was one of seven anxiety-like traits measured. Only dogs that showed it often or very often counted.

Prevalence of anxiety-related traits in 13,715 Finnish dogs (Salonen et al., 2020)
TraitShare of dogs
Noise sensitivity32%
Fear (of people, dogs or new situations)29%
Aggression14%
Separation-related behaviourAbout 5%

The authors point out that earlier studies found separation anxiety in 14–20% of dogs, two to three times higher than their result. They suggest the difference comes from their stricter cut-off, since they counted only dogs that showed the behaviour frequently. They also note that their sample was self-selected, so it may not represent all dogs. Taken together, a fair reading is that frequent, serious separation problems affect around one dog in twenty, and milder or occasional distress is more common.

Are Standard Poodles more prone to it?

You will find websites quoting precise Poodle percentages and odds ratios. We could not trace any of them to a published study, so we do not repeat them. The Finnish study analysed breeds with more than 200 responses each, and Poodles were not among them. In that study, separation-related behaviour was most common in mixed-breed dogs and Soft-Coated Wheaten Terriers.

What the breed sources do say is about temperament. The AKC breed profile describes Poodles as very active dogs that need daily exercise and enjoy keeping busy. Hill's breed guide lists the Poodle as less suitable for people who want a dog that can be left alone for long periods. Neither says Poodles develop separation anxiety more often. Both suggest that a Poodle left alone for long stretches without enough to do is likely to struggle.

Which traits go together with separation problems?

The Finnish data is more useful here than any breed label. Dogs with separation-related behaviour were:

  • 4.1 times more often hyperactive or impulsive than dogs without it,
  • 3.4 times more often inattentive, and
  • 2.8 times more often fearful.

Separation-related behaviour was also 1.4 times more common in noise-sensitive dogs, and slightly more common in males. The paper cites an earlier Australian survey (McGreevy and Masters, 2008) in which excitable dogs had 9.8 times higher odds of separation distress. These are associations, not causes, but they give a practical hint: a busy, easily wound-up Poodle that also startles at noises is the one to watch, and calm, structured routines are worth building early.

Separation distress or something else?

The ASPCA lists the usual signs as urinating or defecating, barking or howling, chewing and digging at doors, trying to escape, and pacing, when they happen only when the dog is alone or separated from its owner. Many anxious dogs get agitated as their owner gets ready to leave, and the distress usually starts within minutes of the door closing.

What you seePoints to separation distressPoints to something else

The ASPCA also lists medical problems to rule out first when a house-trained dog starts having accidents, so a vet visit is the right first step in that case.

What can trigger it?

The ASPCA says there is no conclusive evidence for exactly why dogs develop separation anxiety, but it names common triggers: a change of owner or family (including time in a shelter), a move to a new home, the loss of a household member, and an abrupt change in schedule. Its example is an owner who has worked from home and then takes a job that leaves the dog alone for six or more hours at a time. For a people-focused dog like a Standard Poodle, that last one is worth planning for. If you know your routine is about to change, start practising short absences weeks before it does.

How to build up time alone, step by step

The ASPCA's approach for moderate and severe cases is desensitisation and counterconditioning: many short separations that do not cause anxiety, lengthened very gradually over weeks of daily sessions. It stresses that this needs care and, ideally, a trained professional, because pushing too fast makes the fear worse. In outline:

  1. Take the sting out of departure cues. If your Poodle gets upset when you pick up keys or put on shoes, do those things several times a day without leaving: put your coat on and watch TV, pick up your keys and sit down.
  2. Practise out-of-sight stays inside. Ask for a sit or down and step behind an inside door, such as the bathroom, for a moment. Move on to a bedroom door, then an exit door you don't normally use.
  3. Add very short absences. The ASPCA suggests starting with one or two seconds out of sight. Once your dog handles five to ten seconds, give it a stuffed food toy just before you step out, as a safety cue.
  4. Wait for calm between reps. Leave a few minutes between absences, and only go again when your dog is completely relaxed.
  5. Grow slowly to 40 minutes. Most anxious reactions happen in the first 40 minutes, so the ASPCA advises building up to that point by a few seconds per session. Once your dog handles 40 minutes, larger jumps are usually fine.

During this training, the ASPCA says the dog should not be left alone except in practice sessions. Its suggestions for covering the gaps include taking the dog to work, using a dog sitter or doggy day care, or leaving it with a friend.

Exercise, brain work and calm greetings

The ASPCA recommends at least 30 minutes of aerobic activity a day, such as running or swimming, ideally right before you leave, so the dog is ready to rest. It also suggests food puzzle toys, chew items, varied walks and reward-based training classes, noting that chewing and licking have a calming effect. Our guide to how much exercise a Standard Poodle needs has a day planner that puts the hard exercise before the longest absence.

Keep hellos and goodbyes boring. The ASPCA suggests a quick pat and "goodbye" when you leave, and ignoring your dog on return until it settles.

A calm grooming routine helps too. Short, predictable sessions teach a Poodle to relax while being handled, which carries over to other parts of the day. What not to do

  • Don't punish. The ASPCA is clear: these are distress responses, not disobedience or spite, and punishment can make them much worse.
  • Don't assume a crate fixes it. A crate can be a safe place for some dogs and add stress for others. The ASPCA suggests watching your dog in the crate while you are home first.
  • Don't rush the ladder. The ASPCA warns that many owners make this error: they want quick progress, leave for too long, and make the anxiety worse.

When to get professional help

If your Poodle injures itself trying to escape, cannot cope with even a few seconds alone, or is getting worse despite careful practice, contact a certified professional. The ASPCA points owners to veterinary behaviourists, certified applied animal behaviourists and certified professional dog trainers, and suggests a vet visit first whenever house soiling is involved.

Frequently Asked Questions

How common is separation anxiety in dogs?

About 5% of 13,715 dogs showed frequent separation-related behaviour in the 2020 Finnish study in Scientific Reports. Earlier studies cited there found 14–20% with looser definitions.

Are Standard Poodles prone to separation anxiety?

There is no reliable Poodle-specific figure. Hill's says Poodles suit people who don't want to leave a dog alone for long periods, and the AKC describes them as very active dogs that need daily exercise.

How do I know it's separation anxiety and not boredom?

The ASPCA says separation distress happens only when the dog is alone or about to be, usually starts within minutes, and often comes with anxiety while you get ready to leave. Bored dogs usually don't look anxious.

How long does it take to fix?

The ASPCA says there are no standard timelines. Absences grow by a few seconds per session over weeks, depending on how your dog copes.

Will a crate help?

For some dogs it is a safe place; for others it adds stress. Test it while you are home and watch for panting, drooling or frantic escape attempts.

Can a dog sitter or day care help?

Yes, while you train. The ASPCA says most dogs with separation anxiety are fine as long as someone is with them, and that person doesn't have to be you.

Does exercise help?

Yes, as part of the plan. The ASPCA recommends at least 30 minutes of aerobic exercise daily, ideally before you leave.

The bottom line

Serious separation problems affect a minority of dogs, around 5% in the largest recent survey, and there is no trustworthy figure that shows Standard Poodles are worse. Busy, excitable and noise-sensitive dogs are the ones to watch. If yours struggles, check that the distress really happens only when you are gone, then build up absences in tiny steps, with plenty of exercise and no punishment.
